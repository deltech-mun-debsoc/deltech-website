FAA ground stop was a
network fault, not AI

A nationwide ground stop halted US departures for over four hours on 13 November, days after the
FAA took its AI-based SMART tool national. The FAA blames an unstable legacy network, not AI.

The FAA says data flooded its network at roughly
1,000 connection attempts every four to five seconds.
Early findings point to a radar set in Amarillo, Texas
and a radio site in El Dorado, Arkansas, where
provider L3Harris had been fitting a new router. Both
sites were blocked and the network settled.
SMART, built by Air Space Intelligence, went
nationwide three days earlier. The FAA calls it a data
aggregator that feeds nothing into air traffic control,
and the company says it played no part. The FAA and
L3Harris are still probing the root cause.

Timing is not causation: the
AI tool launched days
earlier, but the fault sat in
old network gear.

Why it Matters

At about 3:30 pm ET on 13 November, the FAA's
legacy network was unstable, disrupting radar feeds
and aircraft communications. A nationwide ground
stop followed. About 3,500 aircraft in the air landed
safely, and controllers kept some frequencies and
radar feeds throughout.
